## Transcript Notes

This transcript introduces the professional fiduciary scenario — a client who has no one in his personal life willing and able to serve as trustee, executor, or DPOA agent. The system must recognize the professional fiduciary as fundamentally different from a personal fiduciary: there are fee structures, "to be named" placeholders, compensation provisions, replacement mechanisms, and the trust protector concept.

Ray is 74, articulate, and deeply motivated by the traumatic experience of his partner Walter dying without legal protections. He speaks carefully but gets emotional when discussing Walter. The conversation includes a named collection bequest to a public institution, multiple missing or outdated beneficiary designations, and institutional beneficiaries in backup fiduciary roles.

**ATTORNEY:** Ray, good morning. Come in, please. Can I get you anything?

**ATTORNEY:** Tea it is. So before we begin — the standard disclaimer. We're going to cover some topics that can feel heavy. What happens when you're gone, who manages things, end-of-life wishes. If you need a break at any time —

**RAY:** I won't need a break. I've been thinking about this every day for six years. It's time to actually do it.

**ATTORNEY:** Six years is a long time to carry that. What happened six years ago?

**RAY:** Walter died. My partner. We were together twenty-eight years. He got cancer — it was stomach cancer, and by the time they found it, it was everywhere. He died in 2018. And the experience of trying to manage his affairs afterward... it was the worst thing I've ever been through, and I've been through his death.

**ATTORNEY:** I'm so sorry. Can you tell me more about what made it so difficult?

**RAY:** Walter and I never formalized anything. We never got married — we kept saying we would, after it became legal, but we just never got around to it. No trust, no will, no powers of attorney. His brother showed up at the hospital claiming to be the next of kin. I had to petition for conservatorship. It took months. I spent thousands of dollars. *[Ray pauses, composing himself]*

**RAY:** I am not going to let that happen to anyone on my account. That's why I'm here.

**ATTORNEY:** That is an incredibly powerful reason, and I'm honored you're trusting me with this. My own mom went through something similar when my dad... well, when things were complicated. I understand the urgency.

**RAY:** Thank you.

**ATTORNEY:** And friends?

**RAY:** My closest friend is Evelyn. Evelyn Marsh. She's 71 — we worked together at the library for years. We have lunch every Tuesday. She's wonderful. But Evelyn has her own health issues — she had a hip replacement last year, and she's got early-stage macular degeneration. She's told me, very directly, that she loves me but she cannot take on the responsibility of being my trustee or my agent. She said, and I quote, "Ray, I can barely manage my own affairs."

**ATTORNEY:** Anyone else?

**RAY:** My neighbor, Joseph. Joseph Kim. He's 45, very kind. He checks on me, helps me with things around the condo — taking out the trash when my back acts up, that sort of thing. **ATTORNEY:** So you don't have anyone in your personal life who is both willing and able to take on the trustee, executor, or power of attorney roles?

**RAY:** That is correct. And I've made peace with that. It's sad, but it's reality. Which is why I was told I should look into hiring a professional fiduciary.

**[10:00] — Professional Fiduciary Discussion**

**ATTORNEY:** Tell me what you know about professional fiduciaries.

**RAY:** Not much. I know they're licensed in California. I know they manage estates and trusts for people who don't have family to do it. My previous attorney — the one I consulted briefly about Walter's conservatorship — she mentioned it. She said there are people whose job this is.

**ATTORNEY:** That's essentially right. A licensed professional fiduciary in California can serve as your trustee, executor, and even your power of attorney agent. They're regulated by the Professional Fiduciaries Bureau. They charge fees — typically either a percentage of assets under management, an hourly rate, or some combination. The advantage is that this is their profession. They handle estate administration for a living. The disadvantage is that they don't know you personally the way a family member would.

**RAY:** I understand that. And honestly, I'd rather have a competent stranger than a well-meaning friend who's in over their head. Evelyn would try her best, but she'd be overwhelmed.

**ATTORNEY:** Have you met with any professional fiduciaries yet?

**RAY:** Not yet. I wanted to get the plan set up first so I know what I'm asking them to do. Then I can interview a few and pick one.

**ATTORNEY:** That's a reasonable approach. For now, we'll structure the documents with a placeholder — "professional fiduciary to be named" — and you can fill in the specific person once you've made your selection. The trust will include provisions for reasonable compensation and a process for replacing the fiduciary if they're not performing well.

**RAY:** That's important. I don't want to be locked into someone who turns out to be incompetent. How does replacement work?

**ATTORNEY:** That's actually where I'd recommend including a trust protector provision. A trust protector is someone who has limited authority — they can't manage the trust day to day, but they can step in to remove and replace the professional fiduciary if there's a problem. It's a supervisory role. The trust protector doesn't need to do anything unless something goes wrong.

**RAY:** So who would be my trust protector?

**ATTORNEY:** It should be someone you trust who is willing to take on a very light-touch role. Evelyn might actually be perfect for this — she's not comfortable being trustee, but a trust protector only needs to act if the fiduciary is failing. It's an emergency lever, not a day-to-day job.

**RAY:** Hmm. I could ask Evelyn about that. She might be willing to do that much. And if she can't?

**ATTORNEY:** One option is naming an institutional representative — for example, the executive director of the San Diego LGBT Community Center, in their capacity as a representative of the organization, not as an individual. That way even if personnel change, the role persists.

**RAY:** That's clever. I like that as a backup. The Community Center has been good to me over the years.

**[16:00] — Assets**

**ATTORNEY:** Savings accounts and brokerage accounts can have pay-on-death or transfer-on-death designations. Do you know if you set those up?

**ATTORNEY:** So there are no TOD or POD designations. We'll add those once we know who should receive what. Any life insurance?

**ATTORNEY:** Vehicles?
